HD 189733 b

HD 189733 b is blue in reflected visible light. It circles its star in just over two days, surrounded by a hot atmosphere where telescopes have detected water vapour, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulphide.

How we found a blue world

HD 189733 b’s blue colour was measured with Hubble. In 2013, Thomas Evans and colleagues used Hubble to watch the planet disappear behind its star. The system lost more light in the blue part of the spectrum than at longer visible wavelengths. By measuring the missing light, they worked out the colour of the planet’s reflected light. Their results fit reflective clouds, with sodium absorbing some of the longer-wavelength light.

Evans et al. (2013)

Does it really rain glass sideways?

That famous description brings together two ideas: mineral particles in the atmosphere and powerful winds. Silicate particles could scatter the light. They belong to the same broad family of materials used to make glass. If such particles form and settle, winds could carry them along. But the blue-colour measurement did not identify falling glass, and no one has observed a sideways glass shower.

Evans et al. (2013)

Measuring wind from starlight

The evidence for moving air comes from a different experiment. Tom Louden and Peter Wheatley studied sodium absorption as the planet crossed its star. The absorption shifted in wavelength on opposite edges of the planet, showing gas moving towards and away from us. Their 2015 analysis was consistent with planetary rotation and a strong eastward jet. The measurement tracks moving gas. It gives no speed or direction for falling particles.

Louden & Wheatley (2015)

What Webb added

In 2024, Guangwei Fu and colleagues used Webb to identify water vapour, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulphide in light passing through the atmosphere. These gases absorb different infrared wavelengths, leaving patterns in the spectrum. The inferred abundances indicate more heavy elements in the atmosphere than in the star. These elements came from material collected as the planet formed.

Fu et al. (2024)

HD 209458 b

HD 209458 b is a hot gas giant with sodium in its atmosphere. The sodium absorbs a small part of the starlight passing through the gas. This was the first exoplanet whose atmosphere was detected and chemically identified.

The 2002 sodium detection

During a transit, a little starlight passes through the rim of this planet’s atmosphere. Charbonneau and colleagues found an extra dip at the colours absorbed by sodium. That small difference, reported in 2002, became the first chemical identification of an exoplanet atmosphere. Later observations also detected water vapour.

WASP-39 b

WASP-39 b has roughly the mass of Saturn, spread across a globe larger than Jupiter. Its hot, hydrogen-rich atmosphere contains water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide. Beneath the clouds, the gas grows denser with depth, with no solid ground to stand on.

Sunlight changes the chemistry

Webb caught starlight filtering through WASP-39 b and found a strong carbon-dioxide fingerprint. A second, initially puzzling feature pointed to sulphur dioxide. Photochemical models explained how stellar ultraviolet light could start reactions that make it. The spectrum therefore tells us both what is in the air and something about the chemistry happening there.

WASP-107 b

WASP-107 b has an enormous atmosphere for its modest mass. Almost as wide as Jupiter, it has only about a tenth of Jupiter’s mass. Its clouds contain tiny silicate particles, made from the same family of minerals that forms much of Earth’s rock. The particles remain suspended high in the hot gas.

Silicates and a methane shortage

The broad infrared feature measured by Webb’s MIRI instrument fits tiny silicate grains suspended in the atmosphere. A later NIRSpec spectrum also detected methane, but far less than expected in gas at the upper atmosphere’s temperature. Models connect that shortage to mixing from a hotter interior. Models combine the measured gases, mass and size to estimate the heat keeping the atmosphere inflated.

WASP-43 b

WASP-43 b completes an orbit in about 20 hours and is expected to keep the same side facing its star. Its day-side atmosphere reaches roughly 1,250 °C, while even the night side is around 590 °C. Thick clouds probably cover the darker hemisphere, hiding the hotter gas below.

Day and night in infrared

Astronomers watched WASP-43 b throughout an orbit with Webb. As its bright and dark hemispheres turned into view, the changing infrared light showed a hot day side and a much fainter night side. Models with clouds on the night side fit the observations: those clouds hide warmer gas underneath. The infrared light comes from hot atmospheric gas.

WASP-121 b

The day side of WASP-121 b is so hot that some ingredients of rock remain in gas form. Its atmosphere contains silicon monoxide, a gas made of silicon and oxygen. The cooler night side contains methane, giving this giant different mixtures of gases on opposite sides.

Chemistry changes across the globe

Webb can compare the light arriving from this planet’s day and night hemispheres. The spectra show silicon monoxide and a different mixture of gases on each hemisphere, including methane on the cooler night side. At these temperatures, material that would form rock on Earth can remain in the air. Separate helium observations follow gas escaping much farther from the planet.

WASP-17 b

WASP-17 b is nearly twice as wide as Jupiter, with less than half its mass. Tiny quartz particles form clouds high in its atmosphere. These crystals are far smaller than grains of sand, suspended above a vast envelope of hot gas.

The quartz absorption feature

Quartz is familiar as a solid crystal on Earth. On WASP-17 b, models fit the observations with particles much smaller than sand suspended high in hot gas. Grant and colleagues found an absorption feature near 8.6 micrometres in Webb data. Cloud models containing tiny quartz grains reproduce its shape.

GJ 1214 b

GJ 1214 b is wrapped in thick clouds and haze that reflect much of its star’s light. They conceal a deep atmosphere and whatever lies beneath it. A water-rich interior is one possibility, with water buried under pressures far beyond those in Earth’s oceans.

Clouds obscure the spectrum

For years, GJ 1214 b’s transmission spectra showed few distinct absorption features. Thick aerosols can obscure the gas below and wash out its chemical fingerprints. Webb then watched the planet through an orbit in infrared light. Its changing brightness constrained how it moves heat and reflects starlight, while its deeper atmosphere remains hidden.

LHS 3844 b

Dark rock is exposed to the nearby star on LHS 3844 b, which appears to have little atmosphere. Basalt is one possible surface material, either in slabs or weathered dust. The planet completes an orbit in about 11 hours.

Comparing hot rock spectra

Rock gives off infrared light with a pattern that depends on its minerals. Observations of LHS 3844 b can be compared with laboratory spectra of heated rocks. The Webb study finds a dark, largely featureless spectrum compatible with low-silica surface materials. Basalt is one candidate. The data allow both exposed slabs and weathered powder, so the texture of the ground remains an open question.

TOI-561 b

TOI-561 b circles its star in less than 11 hours. A thick atmosphere may surround this intensely heated rocky world, above an ocean of molten rock. Gases dissolved in the molten interior could be released into the air, helping replenish what escapes to space.

Air above a lava world

Webb measured the day-side infrared spectrum of TOI-561 b and found it cooler than the tested bare-rock cases. A thick atmosphere is a promising explanation. Models then ask how gas could survive so close to the star: a molten interior can store and release it over time. The models produce carbon-dioxide-rich and sulphur-dioxide-rich envelopes. Neither composition has been measured on this planet.

Kepler-16 b

Kepler-16 b travels around two stars in a 229-day year. The planet is similar to Saturn in mass and size. Its two suns move around each other while the planet follows a much wider path.

TRAPPIST-1 b

TRAPPIST-1 b is the innermost of seven known planets around a tiny, cool star. Its hot day side and much colder night side suggest exposed rock with little atmosphere to carry heat between them.

A faint night side

A substantial atmosphere can carry heat away from a planet’s day side. Webb’s infrared phase observations instead find a hot day side on TRAPPIST-1 b and no significant night-side emission. That pattern fits exposed rock with little air to move the heat. The other planets receive less starlight, providing comparisons for studies of atmospheric loss.

51 Peg b

51 Peg b is a hot giant with water vapour in its atmosphere. It circles a Sun-like star in just over four days. Because it does not transit the star, its physical size is harder to measure.

The 1995 discovery

Mayor and Queloz found a repeating wobble in a Sun-like star in 1995. The four-day rhythm was caused by a giant planet much closer to its star than the giants of our Solar System. Since 51 Peg b does not transit, its radius is harder to establish. Modern high-resolution spectra can separate the moving planet’s atmospheric signature from the star’s light.

Proxima Centauri b

Proxima Centauri b circles the nearest star to the Sun in about eleven days. Its minimum mass is close to Earth’s. We still do not know whether this small world has an atmosphere or a liquid-water surface.

An eleven-day signal

A repeating change in Proxima Centauri’s velocity led to the detection of a planet on an eleven-day orbit. That wobble gives a minimum mass because the orbit’s tilt is uncertain. The planet receives a level of starlight compatible with liquid water under suitable atmospheric conditions, but its atmosphere and radius have not been measured.

Anglada-Escudé et al. 2016

Outer rock

Its minimum mass is compatible with a rocky planet. No atmosphere or surface has been detected, and its radius is still unknown.
