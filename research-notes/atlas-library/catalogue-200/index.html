HD 189733 b

HD 189733 b is blue in reflected visible light. It circles its star in just over two days, surrounded by a hot atmosphere where telescopes have detected water vapour, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide.

How we found a blue world

The striking thing about HD 189733 b is that its blue colour comes from a measurement. In 2013, Thomas Evans and colleagues used Hubble to watch the planet disappear behind its star. The system lost more light in the blue part of the spectrum than at longer visible wavelengths. By measuring the missing light, they worked out the colour of the planet’s reflected light. Their results fit reflective clouds, with sodium absorbing some of the longer-wavelength light.

Evans et al. (2013)

Does it really rain glass sideways?

That famous description brings together two ideas: mineral particles in the atmosphere and powerful winds. Silicate particles—made from the same broad family of materials used to make glass—could help scatter the light. If such particles form and settle, winds could carry them along. But the blue-colour measurement did not identify falling glass, and no one has observed a sideways glass shower.

Evans et al. (2013)

Measuring wind from starlight

The evidence for moving air comes from a different experiment. Tom Louden and Peter Wheatley studied sodium absorption as the planet crossed its star. The absorption shifted in wavelength on opposite edges of the planet, revealing gas moving towards and away from us. Their 2015 analysis was consistent with planetary rotation and a strong eastward jet. It measured atmospheric motion, rather than the speed or direction of falling particles.

Louden & Wheatley (2015)

What Webb added

In 2024, Guangwei Fu and colleagues used Webb to identify water vapour, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide in light passing through the atmosphere. These gases absorb different infrared wavelengths, leaving patterns in the spectrum. Together, their inferred abundances point to an atmosphere enriched in heavier elements relative to its star—a clue to the material the planet gathered as it formed.

Fu et al. (2024)

HD 209458 b

HD 209458 b is a hot gas giant with sodium in its atmosphere. The sodium absorbs a small part of the starlight passing through the gas. This was the first exoplanet whose atmosphere was detected and chemically identified.

A shadow with a chemical fingerprint

During a transit, a little starlight passes through the rim of this planet’s atmosphere. Charbonneau and colleagues found an extra dip at the colours absorbed by sodium. That small difference, reported in 2002, became the first chemical identification of an exoplanet atmosphere. Later observations also detected water vapour.

WASP-39 b

WASP-39 b has roughly the mass of Saturn, spread across a globe larger than Jupiter. Its hot, hydrogen-rich atmosphere contains water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide. Beneath the clouds, the gas grows denser with depth, with no solid ground to stand on.

Sunlight changes the chemistry

Webb caught starlight filtering through WASP-39 b and found a strong carbon-dioxide fingerprint. A second, initially puzzling feature pointed to sulfur dioxide. Photochemical models explained how stellar ultraviolet light could start reactions that make it. The spectrum therefore tells us both what is in the air and something about the chemistry happening there.

WASP-107 b

WASP-107 b has an enormous atmosphere for its modest mass. Almost as wide as Jupiter, it has only about a tenth of Jupiter’s mass. Its clouds contain tiny silicate particles, made from the same family of minerals that forms much of Earth’s rock. Here, that material hangs high in a hot atmosphere.

Rock in the clouds, heat from below

The broad infrared feature measured by Webb’s MIRI instrument fits tiny silicate grains suspended in the atmosphere. A later NIRSpec spectrum also detected methane, but far less than expected in gas at the upper atmosphere’s temperature. Models connect that shortage to mixing from a hotter interior. Combining the gases with the planet’s mass and size gives clues to what keeps this enormous atmosphere inflated.

WASP-43 b

WASP-43 b completes an orbit in about 20 hours and is expected to keep the same side facing its star. Its day-side atmosphere reaches roughly 1,250 °C, while even the night side is around 590 °C. Thick clouds probably cover the darker hemisphere, hiding the hotter gas below.

Following a whole day and night

Astronomers watched WASP-43 b throughout an orbit with Webb. As its bright and dark hemispheres turned into view, the changing infrared light revealed a hot day side and a much fainter night side. Models with clouds on the night side fit the observations: those clouds hide warmer gas underneath. The infrared observations measure the atmosphere’s heat, rather than a solid surface.

WASP-121 b

The day side of WASP-121 b is so hot that some ingredients of rock remain in gas form. Its atmosphere contains silicon monoxide, a gas made of silicon and oxygen. The cooler night side contains methane, giving this giant different mixtures of gases on opposite sides.

Two sides of an extreme atmosphere

Webb can compare the light arriving from this planet’s day and night hemispheres. The spectra reveal silicon monoxide and a striking chemical contrast, including methane on the cooler night side. At these temperatures, material that would form rock on Earth can remain in the air. Separate helium observations follow gas escaping much farther from the planet.

WASP-17 b

WASP-17 b is nearly twice as wide as Jupiter, with less than half its mass. Tiny quartz particles form clouds high in its atmosphere. These crystals are far smaller than grains of sand, suspended above a vast envelope of hot gas.

GJ 1214 b

GJ 1214 b is wrapped in thick clouds and haze that reflect much of its star’s light. They conceal a deep atmosphere and whatever lies beneath it. A water-rich interior is one possibility, with water buried under pressures far beyond those in Earth’s oceans.

The planet that hides its atmosphere

For years, GJ 1214 b produced frustratingly flat transmission spectra. Thick aerosols can obscure the gas below and wash out its chemical fingerprints. Webb then watched the planet through an orbit in infrared light. Its changing brightness constrained how it moves heat and reflects starlight, while its deeper atmosphere remains hidden.

LHS 3844 b

A year on LHS 3844 b lasts about 11 hours. This small rocky world appears to have little atmosphere, leaving its surface exposed to its nearby star. Dark rock such as basalt could cover the ground, either in solid slabs or broken down into weathered dust.

Reading the ground by its heat

Rock gives off infrared light with a pattern that depends on its minerals. Observations of LHS 3844 b can be compared with laboratory spectra of heated rocks. The Webb study finds a dark, largely featureless spectrum compatible with low-silica surface materials. Basalt is one candidate. The data allow both exposed slabs and weathered powder, so the texture of the ground remains an open question.

A substantial atmosphere can carry heat away from a planet’s day side. Webb’s infrared phase observations instead find a hot day side on TRAPPIST-1 b and no significant night-side emission. That pattern fits exposed rock with little air to move the heat. Comparing b with its neighbouring planets lets astronomers test how rocky worlds around the same star retain or lose atmospheres.

Measuring the spin of a distant world

A rotating globe sends light toward us from one side and away from us from the other. The resulting Doppler shifts broaden lines in its spectrum. High-resolution observations of Beta Pictoris b measure that broadening in molecular features. A spin period can then be estimated using a radius and a viewing angle. The estimated nine-hour day depends on the assumed radius and tilt.

The hot giant that changed the search

Proxima Centauri b

Proxima Centauri b circles the nearest star to the Sun in about eleven days. Its minimum mass is close to Earth’s. We still do not know whether this small world has an atmosphere or a liquid-water surface.

A world around the nearest star

Proxima Centauri’s small, repeating velocity changes revealed a planet on an eleven-day orbit. That wobble gives a minimum mass because the orbit’s tilt is uncertain. The planet receives a level of starlight compatible with liquid water under suitable atmospheric conditions, but its atmosphere and radius have not been measured.

Anglada-Escudé et al. 2016
