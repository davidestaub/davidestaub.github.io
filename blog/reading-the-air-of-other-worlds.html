01 · The census

A galaxy full of strange worlds

Three decades ago, humanity knew exactly eight planets — the ones orbiting our own Sun. Today we have confirmed more than 6,000 planets around other stars, and the surprise isn't just the number. It's the variety. Most of them look like nothing in our Solar System.

~30%
Gas giants

Massive, hydrogen-rich worlds like Jupiter — some so close to their star that their year lasts days.

~35%
Neptune-like

Icy, gaseous planets. Common out there — and oddly, mostly absent in our own Solar System's inner reaches.

~31%
Super-Earths

Rocky worlds larger than Earth but smaller than Neptune. We have no local example at all.

~4%
Terrestrial

Small and rocky, like Earth or Mars. Rare in the catalogue — mostly because they're the hardest to find.

🔭
A planet census is one thing. But the question everyone actually wants answered is: what is it like on these worlds? Is there water? Clouds? Air at all?

02 · The hunt

Thirty years from discovery to weather report

Finding a planet and characterising it are two different games. The first thirty years were mostly about counting. Only recently did we get the tools to ask what these planets are actually made of.

1990 — Hubble launches

The space-telescope era begins

Hubble wasn't built for exoplanets — none were known yet around normal stars — but it would later make the first detection of an exoplanet atmosphere.

1995 — 51 Pegasi b

The first planet around a Sun-like star

The ELODIE spectrograph caught the star 51 Pegasi wobbling — tugged by an unseen giant planet racing around it every 4.2 days. A hot Jupiter: the first of many worlds with no Solar System counterpart. It earned a Nobel Prize.

1999 — HD 209458 b

The first transit

For the first time, a planet was caught crossing in front of its star, dimming it by about 1.5%. This "transit method" would soon become astronomy's planet-finding workhorse — and, as we'll see, the key to reading atmospheres.

2009 — Kepler

Counting worlds by the thousand

Kepler stared at one patch of sky for years, watching 150,000 stars for tiny transit dips. It alone confirmed over 2,700 planets and taught us that planets outnumber stars.

2018 — TESS

Surveying the whole sky

TESS scans almost the entire sky for transiting planets around nearby, bright stars — exactly the targets big telescopes can study in detail.

2021 — JWST

The characterisation machine

The James Webb Space Telescope changed the game: a 6.5-metre gold mirror in deep space, sensitive enough to measure not just that a planet transits, but how the dip changes with the colour of light. That difference is where atmospheres hide.

03 · The trick

Try it: fly a planet across its star

Here's a star, a planet, and a light meter. When the planet crosses the stellar disk, it blocks a tiny fraction of the light. That dip in brightness is a transit — and everything below follows from it.

🔭
Drag the sliders! Make the planet bigger and watch the dip deepen — the depth tells us the planet's size.
The planet blocks 1.0% of the starlight. Now try the wavelength slider — with an atmosphere, the dip depth changes with colour…
🔭
Here's the magic: a planet's atmosphere is opaque at some colours and transparent at others. Water vapour, for example, absorbs strongly around 1.4 and 1.9 µm — at those colours the planet looks slightly bigger, so the dip is slightly deeper.
🔭
Press “Scan the rainbow”: measure the transit depth at every colour, plot depth against wavelength, and you get a transmission spectrum — a chemical fingerprint of air on a world we will never visit.

04 · The real thing

What JWST actually sees

This is a stylised version of a real measurement: the transmission spectrum of WASP-17 b, a hot giant planet whose transit JWST and Hubble have measured across wavelengths. The bumps are not noise — they are water vapour in an alien sky.

H₂O — water vapour CO₂ — carbon dioxide CO — carbon monoxide CH₄ — methane

Each molecule absorbs at its own characteristic wavelengths, so each leaves a distinct pattern of bumps. JWST has now detected all of the above in exoplanet atmospheres.

🔭
One transit of a typical hot Jupiter changes the starlight by about 1% — and the atmospheric fingerprint on top of that is another hundred times smaller. Measuring it means controlling every instrumental effect with exquisite care.

05 · Where I come in

The hard part is trusting the answer

Signals this small put enormous weight on the analysis: subtle instrument systematics and modelling choices can shift the answer by more than the atmospheric signal itself. My PhD research at Imperial College London develops physics-based, differentiable methods to make these measurements — and the conclusions drawn from them — more trustworthy.
