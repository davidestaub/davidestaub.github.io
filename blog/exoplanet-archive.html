RECORD 001 / 55 CNC E

55 Cancri e

A year in less than a day

On 55 Cancri e, a year is over in less than 18 hours. It orbits so close to its star that rock may melt on the dayside. Webb has found signs of an atmosphere. Its glow also changes between observations, and researchers are trying to work out why.

Atmosphere

Webb has found signs of an atmosphere around this intensely hot world. It may contain carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide. Gas escaping from molten rock could help keep the atmosphere supplied.

The system has a second star

55 Cancri has a second star: a small red dwarf called 55 Cancri B. It lies far beyond these planets. From Earth, the gap between the two stars looks about 1,065 times wider than the distance from Earth to the Sun.

How do we know?

The orbit and size

Each time the planet passes in front of its star, the starlight dips. The time between dips tells us the length of its year. The depth of the dip tells us how big the planet is, once we know the star’s size. The planet also makes its star wobble, which helps us estimate its mass.

The numbers here come from Bourrier et al. (2018), through the NASA Exoplanet Archive. They give a year of 17.7 hours and a diameter of about 23,900 kilometres.

The atmospheric evidence

Hu et al. (2024) used Webb to measure the planet’s heat. As the planet slipped behind its star, some infrared light disappeared. That missing light came from the planet.

The pattern across different wavelengths fits an atmosphere containing carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide. Molten rock below could supply gas to this atmosphere.

Inside the planet

The cutaway shows one possible structure. We estimate what might be inside from the planet’s size, weight and heat. The thickness of each layer is approximate.

What is still open

The planet’s glow changes between observations. We are still learning what its atmosphere contains and why its brightness changes. Zhan & Koll (2026) explore an atmosphere rich in carbon dioxide. The spinning globe lets you see the illustration from all sides. It does not show a measured day length.

The landscape and poster imagine what a volcanic surface could look like. We cannot see individual cliffs or lava rivers on this planet.

About the filters

We group 55 Cancri e with rocky planets based on its mass and size. Its temperature estimate is around 2,000 kelvin, or 1,700 °C. Conditions vary across the planet. We use G8 V for its star’s class, following Deibert et al. (2021). Some studies place the star just across the boundary in class K.
