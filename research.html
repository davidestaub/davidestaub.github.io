Research

My PhD is on JWST observations of exoplanets. Before that, I worked on how the brain processes stories, and on neural networks for seismic waves.

Exoplanet atmospheres with JWST

PhD · Imperial College London · ongoing

When a planet passes in front of its star, some of the starlight filters through the planet's atmosphere. JWST measures how much light is blocked at each wavelength, and from that we work out what the atmosphere is made of. The atmospheric signal is tiny, so the result depends on how well the telescope, the detector and the transit itself are accounted for.

This analysis is the focus of my PhD. I will write more here once the first papers are out. Until then, my interactive explainer covers the background, and the Exoplanet Archive collects 40 planets and what is known about them.

How the brain follows a story

HomanLab · Psychiatric University Hospital of Zürich

Following a story takes two things at once: meaning that builds up slowly over many sentences, and quick updates when the story moves to a new scene. We used a large language model to derive one signal for each directly from the story text, without manual annotation: drift for the slow build-up and shift for the jumps at event boundaries.

We compared both signals with 7 Tesla fMRI of one volunteer who listened to more than seven hours of crime stories. Drift predicted activity mainly in hubs of the default-mode network. Shift predicted activity in the primary auditory cortex and in language areas on both sides of the brain. The preprint is on arXiv.

Physics-informed neural networks for seismology

Master's thesis · ETH Zürich · 2024

My master's thesis tested physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) on the elastic wave equation, which describes how seismic waves travel through the Earth. Networks with the wave physics built into their layers (wavelet and plane-wave layers, with an encoder and a decoder) were about twice as accurate as standard PINNs.

Conditioning a network on the position of the seismic source gives the wavefield for any source position in a single forward pass, much faster than a finite-difference solver. The thesis was featured in the British Seismology Meeting 2024 report in Astronomy & Geophysics, and the full text is in the ETH Research Collection.
